## Introduction

Science is supposed to give us answers. And it does — more answers than any previous generation of humans has ever had. We know the universe is about 13.8 billion years old. We know it started in a hot, dense explosion called the Big Bang. We know it is made mostly of atoms, light, and gravity.

But the more carefully we look, the more we find things that do not fit. Signals that should not exist. Galaxies that formed too fast. Matter that bends light but cannot be touched or seen. A mysterious force pulling entire galaxies through space toward a hidden destination. A radio signal from 1977 that has never been explained.

These are not fringe ideas pushed by people outside of science. Every anomaly in this book is documented by peer-reviewed research — meaning other scientists have checked the work and found it valid. These findings keep serious astronomers and physicists up at night, arguing in journals and at conferences about what they mean.

The twenty-six phenomena described here are arranged roughly from oldest to newest — from puzzles that go back decades to discoveries made just last year. Some have partial answers. Most do not. A few, if solved, would change everything we think we know about the universe.

### A Brief Map of the Cosmos

A short orientation may help before the stories begin. The universe is organized at vastly different scales, and the anomalies in this book touch every one of them.

The smallest building blocks of ordinary matter are **atoms and particles**. Matter has a mirror twin called **antimatter** that annihilates it on contact. **Stars** are giant nuclear furnaces, and when very massive stars die they leave behind either **neutron stars** (extraordinarily dense balls of matter) or **black holes** (regions where gravity is so strong that nothing escapes). A **solar system** is a star and everything bound to it by gravity — planets, moons, asteroids, comets.

**Galaxies** are collections of billions of stars held together by gravity. Our galaxy, the **Milky Way**, has a **supermassive black hole** at its center. **Galaxy clusters** are groups of hundreds or thousands of galaxies bound together by their combined gravity. Clusters are themselves strung along enormous threads and sheets of matter called **filaments**, with huge empty regions called **voids** between them. That pattern is called the **cosmic web**. The **observable universe** is everything we can, in principle, see — a sphere about 93 billion light-years across, centered on Earth only because Earth is the vantage point.

Two ingredients we cannot directly see are **dark matter**, invisible stuff that outweighs ordinary matter by about five to one and is known only through its gravity, and **dark energy**, a mysterious property of space itself that is pushing the universe apart faster and faster, accounting for about 68 percent of the total energy content of the universe. Together, dark matter and dark energy make up roughly 95 percent of the universe's contents. We live inside a universe that is mostly invisible.

### Why Twenty-Six

Why 26 anomalies? The count is arbitrary — it could have been 20, 30, or something else. Since the current year is 2026, it is a convenient number. Coincidentally, it is also the number of letters in the English alphabet, symbolically indicating the intent to be comprehensive while still setting a limit on what is covered.

## 1. The Flatness Problem — the universe balanced on a cosmic knife-edge for 13.8 billion years

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/CMB_Timeline300_no_WMAP.jpg/320px-CMB_Timeline300_no_WMAP.jpg){alt=""}
*A timeline of the universe from the Big Bang to the present. The moment of inflation, about 10 to the negative 30 seconds after the beginning, is what supposedly flattened the universe's geometry.*


*Cosmology, 1960s onward*

Imagine you are walking a tightrope stretched across a canyon. One step too far left or right, and you fall. Now imagine that someone placed you on that tightrope with perfect balance — and you have been walking it for 13.8 billion years without falling. That is essentially what the universe has done.

Physicists describe the shape of the universe using geometry. A universe with too much matter curves in on itself and eventually collapses, like a ball. A universe with too little matter keeps expanding forever, spreading out until it is nearly empty. But our universe turns out to be almost perfectly flat — right on the knife's edge between those two fates.

For the universe to be this flat today, it had to start with almost impossibly precise balance at the moment of the Big Bang. The margin for error was about one part in a trillion trillion trillion trillion trillion. If you tried to achieve that kind of precision by luck, you would have better odds of winning the lottery a thousand times in a row.

Physicists recognized this problem in the late 1960s, and in 1980 a physicist named Alan Guth proposed a solution called cosmic inflation. The idea is that in the first tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang, the universe expanded at an unimaginable speed — far faster than the speed of light. This would have stretched any curves out of the universe the way blowing up a balloon stretches out its wrinkles, leaving it flat.

Most cosmologists accept inflation as the best available explanation. But no one has proven it happened. The main evidence would be a particular pattern in the faint glow of the early universe, called B-mode polarization in the cosmic microwave background. Experiments have been searching for it for years. So far, nothing definitive has appeared.

## 2. The Missing Antimatter — why anything exists at all

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/Andrei_Sakharov_with_Richard_Lourie.jpg/320px-Andrei_Sakharov_with_Richard_Lourie.jpg){alt=""}
*Andrei Sakharov, the Soviet physicist who in 1967 laid out the three conditions that any theory must satisfy to explain why matter — and not pure radiation — survived the Big Bang.*


*Particle physics, 1967*

Here is one of the strangest facts in all of physics: you should not exist.

When the Big Bang happened, it should have created matter and antimatter in equal amounts. Matter and antimatter are mirror images of each other — when they meet, they annihilate in a burst of energy. If equal amounts of both were created, they should have wiped each other out completely, leaving a universe of pure energy and nothing else. No atoms. No stars. No planets. No people.

And yet here we are. Somehow, for every billion pairs that annihilated each other, there was one extra particle of ordinary matter left over. That tiny excess — one in a billion — is every galaxy, every star, every planet, and every living thing in the universe.

Physicist Andrei Sakharov identified in 1967 what conditions would be needed for matter to win this battle. But the Standard Model of particle physics, our best current theory of how matter works at the smallest scales, does not produce enough of an imbalance to explain what we observe.

Scientists have searched for the answer in laboratories like CERN in Switzerland, studying the behavior of particles called B mesons and kaons. They have found small asymmetries between matter and antimatter, but nothing close to what the universe requires. The mechanism that tipped the scales — called baryogenesis — remains completely unknown. This is one of the deepest unanswered questions in all of science, because it is literally asking: why does anything exist?

## 3. Dark Matter — the invisible 27 percent holding galaxies together

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e4/Vera_Rubin_measuring_spectra_%28cropped%29.jpg/320px-Vera_Rubin_measuring_spectra_%28cropped%29.jpg){alt=""}
*Astronomer Vera Rubin at her spectrograph. Her 1970s measurements of galaxy rotation curves provided the first broadly accepted evidence for dark matter.*


*Astronomy, 1933 and 1970s*

In the 1930s, astronomer Fritz Zwicky was measuring how fast galaxies move around inside galaxy clusters — enormous collections of hundreds or thousands of galaxies. He expected the galaxies' speeds to match the amount of visible matter in the cluster. They did not. Not even close. The galaxies were moving so fast that they should have flown apart long ago, like riders on a merry-go-round spinning too fast. Something invisible was holding them together.

Most scientists ignored Zwicky's finding for decades. Then, in the 1970s, astronomer Vera Rubin made careful measurements of how fast stars orbit the center of individual galaxies. The outer stars, far from the bright, dense core, should orbit slowly — the same reason distant planets in our solar system move slowly around the Sun. Instead, Rubin found that outer stars orbit just as fast as inner ones. Something invisible was adding extra gravity all the way out to the galaxy's edges.

The evidence has only grown since then. The pattern of galaxy formation in the universe, the way galaxy clusters bend light from objects behind them, the temperature patterns in the cosmic microwave background — all of it only makes sense if the universe contains about five times more invisible matter than visible matter. Astronomers call it dark matter, and they estimate it makes up roughly 27 percent of the universe's total energy content.

The problem is that no one has ever actually caught a dark matter particle. Physicists built enormous detectors deep underground, filled with super-pure materials designed to catch a particle called a WIMP — a Weakly Interacting Massive Particle. These detectors are so sensitive they can register a single atom bumping another atom. And still, nothing. Year after year of nothing.

Some physicists argue that dark matter is not a particle at all. Maybe gravity just works differently at large scales than Einstein's equations predict — a hypothesis called Modified Newtonian Dynamics, or MOND. But MOND has its own problems, especially when you look at cases like the Bullet Cluster: two galaxy clusters that passed through each other. The hot gas between them slowed down from the collision, but the gravitational mass — detected by its lensing of background light — kept moving. The mass and the gas separated, exactly as you would expect if dark matter were real particles that do not interact with normal matter. MOND has a much harder time explaining this. As of 2026, neither camp has won the argument.

## 4. Dark Energy — the force pushing the universe apart faster and faster

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/Supernova-1994d.jpg/320px-Supernova-1994d.jpg){alt=""}
*A Type Ia supernova (bright dot at lower left) in a distant galaxy. These stellar explosions have nearly uniform peak brightness, which makes them reliable distance markers and revealed the accelerating expansion of the universe in 1998.*


*Cosmology, 1998*

For most of the twentieth century, astronomers assumed that the universe's expansion was slowing down. Gravity, after all, pulls things together. After the Big Bang's initial explosion, gravity should have been gradually applying the brakes.

In 1998, two independent teams of astronomers were measuring the brightness of distant exploding stars called Type Ia supernovae. These supernovae are useful because they always explode with roughly the same brightness, making them reliable distance markers. The farther away a supernova appears, the dimmer it looks — similar to how a streetlight looks fainter when you are far from it. By measuring how dim these distant supernovae were, the astronomers could calculate how far away they were and, from that, how fast the universe had been expanding at different times in the past.

The results stunned the scientific world: the universe's expansion is not slowing down. It is speeding up. Something is pushing galaxies apart faster and faster, overcoming gravity at large scales. That something is called dark energy, and it makes up about 68 percent of the universe — more than all the matter, dark and ordinary, combined.

The problem is that no one knows what dark energy actually is. The leading idea is that it is a property of empty space itself — a fixed amount of energy in every cubic centimeter of the vacuum, called the cosmological constant. Einstein actually proposed this idea in 1917 and later called it his "biggest blunder" — then it turned out to be real, just not in the way he intended.

But the cosmological constant has a severe problem. Quantum physics predicts that the vacuum should be packed with energy from constantly appearing and disappearing particles. When physicists calculate how much energy that should produce, they get a number that is 10^120 times larger than what we observe — a "one" followed by 120 zeros. That is the worst discrepancy between theory and observation in the history of physics.

## 5. The CMB Cold Spot and the Axis of Evil — patterns in the early universe that should not be there

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3c/Ilc_9yr_moll4096.png/320px-Ilc_9yr_moll4096.png){alt=""}
*A full-sky map of the cosmic microwave background from the WMAP satellite. The tiny temperature differences shown in color are the seeds from which galaxies later formed — and also the source of the Cold Spot and Axis of Evil anomalies.*


*Cosmology, 2004*

The cosmic microwave background — usually called the CMB — is the faint glow of light left over from the Big Bang. It fills the entire sky and is almost perfectly uniform. When you map it in detail, you see tiny temperature variations — spots that are slightly warmer or cooler than average. These variations are the seeds of all structure in the universe: the slight density differences that gravity eventually amplified into galaxies, clusters, and the cosmic web.

The CMB should have no preferred direction. The universe, at the largest scales, should look the same in every direction. But when astronomers analyzed the data from the WMAP and Planck satellites, they found two unsettling patterns.

The first is a cold spot — a region of the southern sky that is significantly colder than surrounding areas, and larger than standard physics predicts it should be. The second is even stranger: the largest-scale patterns in the CMB temperature map appear to line up with each other, and with the plane of our own solar system. This alignment is so unexpected that astronomers sarcastically named it the "Axis of Evil" — because it seemed to mock the assumption that the universe has no special directions.

Both anomalies were confirmed by the Planck satellite, which was more precise than WMAP. One proposed explanation for the cold spot is a "supervoid" — an enormous region of space emptier than average, which would cool light passing through it. A supervoid candidate was found in 2015 in the right direction, but it is not unusual enough to fully explain what we see.

Most scientists think the anomalies are probably statistical accidents — with enough data, some patterns will appear by chance. Others are less comfortable with that explanation and wonder whether the universe has a structure, or a history, that our current models are missing.

## 6. The Great Attractor — something enormous pulling our galaxy toward a hidden destination

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/2MASS_LSS_chart-NEW_Nasa.jpg/320px-2MASS_LSS_chart-NEW_Nasa.jpg){alt=""}
*A large-scale map of galaxies in the local universe from the 2MASS survey. The dense region near the center-right contains the Great Attractor and the Shapley Supercluster, toward which our galaxy is being pulled.*


*Astronomy, 1986*

Our galaxy is moving. That is not surprising — everything in the universe moves. But our galaxy is not just moving along with the general expansion of space. It is being pulled, along with thousands of other galaxies, toward a specific region of the sky at about 630 kilometers per second. That is about 1.4 million miles per hour.

Astronomers in the 1970s noticed something odd about the velocities of galaxies in our neighborhood: they were not quite moving the way the expansion of the universe predicted. Something enormous was pulling on them. By the 1980s, a team of researchers led by Alan Dressler confirmed that the source of this pull lies in the direction of the Centaurus and Norma constellations. They called it the Great Attractor.

The catch is that the Great Attractor sits almost directly behind the densest part of our own galaxy's disk — a region so full of dust and gas that optical telescopes cannot see through it. Astronomers call this the Zone of Avoidance. Getting a clear view requires infrared, radio, or X-ray telescopes that can penetrate the Galactic plane.

Over the following decades, astronomers identified a massive concentration of galaxy clusters in that direction — the Norma Cluster and, beyond it, the vast Shapley Supercluster. In 2014, a team mapped an even larger structure called the Laniakea Supercluster, which stretches 500 million light-years and includes our own Milky Way. In 2025, a structure called Quipu was discovered — 68 galaxy clusters chained together across 1.4 billion light-years. The universe is bigger and more organized at large scales than anyone expected. Whether the Great Attractor is fully explained by these known structures, or whether something even larger lurks behind the Zone of Avoidance, is still being debated.

## 7. Fast Radio Bursts — millisecond flashes that outshine the Sun for a day

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/CSIRO_ScienceImage_4350_CSIROs_Parkes_Radio_Telescope_with_moon_in_the_background.jpg/320px-CSIRO_ScienceImage_4350_CSIROs_Parkes_Radio_Telescope_with_moon_in_the_background.jpg){alt=""}
*The Parkes Radio Telescope in Australia, where the first fast radio burst was found in 2001 archival data by astronomer Duncan Lorimer.*


*Radio astronomy, 2007*

On a summer day in 2001, the Parkes Radio Telescope in Australia recorded something that took six years to find: a burst of radio waves lasting just five milliseconds. In those five milliseconds, it released as much energy as the Sun produces in an entire day.

Astronomer Duncan Lorimer found this "Lorimer Burst" in archived data in 2007. It had come from billions of light-years away. At first, many astronomers suspected it was a glitch. Then more bursts were found — from different directions, at different distances, by different telescopes. Fast Radio Bursts, or FRBs, were real. By 2026, thousands have been catalogued.

The puzzle deepened. Some FRBs repeat — the same source fires again and again, sometimes on a schedule. One fires on a roughly 16-day cycle. Others have never repeated despite being monitored for years. In August 2025, the Canadian telescope CHIME detected the brightest FRB ever recorded — nicknamed RBFLOAT — from a source it had been watching daily for seven years without seeing anything. Then the source exploded with a burst far exceeding anything previously measured.

The breakthrough came in 2020, when a FRB was detected from within our own galaxy, traced to a known magnetar — a neutron star with an extraordinarily powerful magnetic field, left over when a massive star died in a supernova. That connected at least some FRBs to magnetars. But it did not explain why some FRBs never repeat, or how a source can be silent for seven years and then fire with record intensity.

FRBs turned out to be useful for something entirely unexpected: mapping the invisible gas between galaxies. As a radio burst travels through space, different wavelengths slow down by different amounts depending on how many electrons they pass through. By measuring this dispersion, astronomers can weigh the gas along the burst's path. FRBs have already helped confirm where the universe's "missing" ordinary matter is hiding — spread through the thin, hot gas between galaxy clusters.

## 8. The Wow! Signal — the 72-second message from Sagittarius that has never repeated

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d3/Wow_signal.jpg/320px-Wow_signal.jpg){alt=""}
*Jerry Ehman's original 1977 printout of the Wow! Signal, with his handwritten note. The sequence of characters records the signal's intensity as the telescope swept across the sky over 72 seconds.*


*Radio astronomy and SETI, 1977*

On the night of August 15, 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio called Big Ear was scanning the sky for signs of extraterrestrial intelligence. It recorded a strong, narrow-band signal lasting 72 seconds — the exact length of time the telescope's beam swept over any point in the sky as Earth rotated.

When astronomer Jerry Ehman saw the printout a few days later, he circled the sequence of characters representing the signal's intensity and wrote "Wow!" in the margin. The signal was 30 times stronger than the background noise. Its frequency was close to 1420 megahertz — the natural frequency of hydrogen atoms, which SETI researchers had long thought would be a logical channel for an alien civilization to use, because it would be familiar to any technological species.

Nobody has been able to explain it. Every natural source astronomers have proposed — passing comets, reflected signals from satellites, interference from Earth technology — has been checked and ruled out. The telescope was pointed in a specific, known direction when the signal arrived, toward a patch of sky in the constellation Sagittarius. The area has been searched repeatedly with far more sensitive equipment over the following decades. Nothing has ever been heard from that direction again.

The Wow! Signal remains the strongest unexplained signal in the history of the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. It is not proof of anything. It is, however, the closest thing we have to a candidate event — and after nearly fifty years, no one has been able to explain it away.

## 9. The Fermi Paradox — the galaxy should be full of civilizations; it sounds empty

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/Enrico_Fermi_1943-49.jpg/320px-Enrico_Fermi_1943-49.jpg){alt=""}
*Physicist Enrico Fermi, who asked at a Los Alamos lunch in 1950: "Where is everybody?" The question became the Fermi Paradox — a central puzzle of modern astrobiology.*


*Astrobiology, 1950*

In 1950, physicist Enrico Fermi was having lunch with colleagues at Los Alamos. The conversation turned to the possibility of alien civilizations, and Fermi suddenly asked a simple question: "Where is everybody?"

The math is straightforward. Our galaxy contains roughly 200 billion stars. Many of them have planets. Many of those planets are in the "habitable zone" where liquid water can exist. Life evolved on Earth relatively quickly once conditions were right — suggesting it is not astronomically rare. And the galaxy is old. Even at speeds far below the speed of light, a civilization could cross the galaxy in a few tens of millions of years — far less than the galaxy's 10-billion-year age.

So where are they? Every telescope, every radio search, every SETI project has found nothing. No radio signals. No megastructures. No detected probes. No alien artifacts on the Moon or Mars or the outer solar system. Silence.

This is the Fermi Paradox: the universe appears to be extraordinarily well-suited to life, and ancient enough that life should be everywhere, yet we see nothing. Proposed explanations range widely. Maybe life is very rare. Maybe intelligence is rare. Maybe technological civilizations reliably destroy themselves. Maybe they exist but deliberately hide from younger species. Maybe they exist but communicate in ways we cannot detect. Maybe we are simply first.

A concept called the "Great Filter" sharpens the puzzle. Somewhere between "lifeless planet" and "galaxy-spanning civilization," there must be a step that almost nothing gets past. If the filter is behind us — if the hard part was getting life started at all, or getting complex cells to form — then we have already beaten the long odds, and our future may be bright. If the filter is ahead of us — if advanced civilizations reliably destroy themselves — then we should be worried.

The James Webb Space Telescope and other instruments are now searching for biosignatures — signs of life, such as oxygen or methane, in the atmospheres of planets around other stars. If we find a biosignature, it will be the first empirical data point on where the filter lies.

## 10. The Lithium Problem — the only element the Big Bang got wrong

*Cosmology, 1980s onward*

Big Bang nucleosynthesis is one of the triumphs of modern cosmology. Within the first few minutes after the Big Bang, the universe was hot and dense enough for nuclear reactions to occur, producing the lightest elements — hydrogen, helium, and tiny amounts of lithium. The theory predicts exactly how much of each should exist. For hydrogen and helium, the predictions match observation beautifully.

For lithium, they do not.

The theory says that the oldest, most unprocessed stars in our galaxy should contain about three times more lithium-7 than astronomers actually measure. The observed abundance has been checked against many different stars in different parts of the galaxy. It consistently comes in too low. This is known as the cosmological lithium problem.

Proposed solutions fall into three broad categories. The first is that the stars themselves destroy some of their lithium over billions of years, through a process called "lithium depletion" in their atmospheres. The problem is that the destruction rate needed to match observations varies more than it should. The second is that our understanding of the nuclear reactions in the early universe is slightly wrong. But the relevant reactions have been measured in laboratories, and the numbers are well constrained. The third is that something unknown — a new particle, a modification to early-universe physics — slightly altered the outcome of nucleosynthesis.

The lithium problem is not as famous as dark matter or dark energy, but it nags at cosmologists because it is the only significant discrepancy between Big Bang nucleosynthesis theory and observation. Either our models of stellar physics are missing something important, or the early universe contained physics we have not yet accounted for.

## 11. The Pioneer Anomaly — when a deviation from gravity turned out to be waste heat

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Pioneer10-11.jpg/320px-Pioneer10-11.jpg){alt=""}
*An artist's illustration of the Pioneer 10 spacecraft, which carried humanity's first plaque message to extraterrestrial intelligence and showed a tiny unexplained deceleration as it left the solar system.*


*Solar system physics, 1980 to 2012*

NASA launched the Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11 spacecraft in 1972 and 1973 to visit Jupiter and Saturn and then continue outward into deep space. As they left the solar system, tracking data showed that both probes were slowing down slightly faster than gravity alone could explain. The anomaly was tiny — a deceleration of only about 8.7 x 10 to the power of negative 10 meters per second squared — but it was consistent, persistent, and completely unexpected.

For three decades, the Pioneer anomaly was a genuine puzzle. Some physicists speculated that it might indicate a deviation from general relativity, a new force of nature, or an effect related to dark matter. The fact that both spacecraft showed the same anomaly ruled out a malfunction in one of them.

In 2012, a team led by Slava Turyshev completed a careful thermal analysis of the spacecraft. Pioneer's plutonium power source and electronics generated heat that radiated outward from the craft. That radiation — light carrying away momentum — produced a small but calculable thrust pushing back against the direction of motion. When they built a detailed model including every heat source and the geometry of the craft, the predicted thrust closely matched the observed anomaly.

The Pioneer anomaly is included here not because it is unsolved but because it illustrates something important about science. For a generation, this anomaly looked like it might be a window onto new physics. Many smart people spent years taking it seriously. The resolution, when it came, was mundane — a careful accounting of waste heat. This is a useful reminder when thinking about the other findings in this book. Some of them will turn out to be new physics. Others will turn out to be overlooked details. We generally cannot tell which is which until the work is done.

## 12. The Solar Corona Heating Problem — why the air above the Sun is hundreds of times hotter than the Sun

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Total_Solar_Eclipse_8-21-17.jpg/320px-Total_Solar_Eclipse_8-21-17.jpg){alt=""}
*The Sun's corona during a total solar eclipse in 2017. This wispy outer atmosphere reaches temperatures of 1 to 3 million degrees Celsius — hundreds of times hotter than the surface just below it.*


*Solar physics, 1940s onward*

The surface of the Sun — the part you see as the bright disk during a safely filtered view — is about 5,500 degrees Celsius. That is extraordinarily hot. But the Sun's corona, the wispy outer atmosphere visible only during a total solar eclipse, is about 1 to 3 million degrees Celsius. The air above the Sun's surface is hundreds of times hotter than the surface itself.

That makes no sense. Heat flows from hot to cold. If you stand near a fireplace, the air farther from the fire is cooler than the air near the flames. If the surface of the Sun is 5,500 degrees and the corona is over a million, something must be adding energy to the corona from somewhere other than the surface.

Physicists have proposed mechanisms for decades. Two main candidates have emerged. The first is magnetic reconnection — small, rapid rearrangements of the Sun's twisted magnetic field that dump energy into the corona. The second is a type of wave called an Alfvén wave, traveling up from the surface and dissipating energy in the corona.

In 2018, NASA launched the Parker Solar Probe, a spacecraft designed to fly closer to the Sun than any mission before it, dipping into the corona itself. Its data, gathered over multiple close passes, has supported both mechanisms: the probe has detected magnetic reconnection events and Alfvén waves, and each appears to contribute to coronal heating.

As of 2026, the consensus is that the heating involves a combination of processes rather than a single mechanism. But the relative importance of each contributor is still actively debated, and a fully quantitative model that reproduces observed coronal temperatures from first principles does not yet exist.

## 13. Saturn's Rings — the most iconic sight in the solar system may be younger than the dinosaurs

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c7/Saturn_during_Equinox.jpg/320px-Saturn_during_Equinox.jpg){alt=""}
*Saturn and its rings photographed during equinox by the Cassini spacecraft. Cassini's final measurements in 2017 suggested the rings may be no more than a few hundred million years old.*


*Planetary science, 2019*

Saturn's rings are one of the most iconic sights in the solar system. For centuries, astronomers assumed they were as old as Saturn itself — roughly 4.5 billion years, formed along with the planet from the disk of material that built the solar system.

In 2019, analysis of data from NASA's Cassini mission overturned this. In its final dives before plunging into Saturn, Cassini measured the mass of the ring system and the rate at which material is falling out of the rings into the planet. Both measurements pointed to rings much younger than Saturn itself — somewhere between 10 million and 400 million years old. Maybe as recent as the age of the dinosaurs on Earth.

If the rings are young, they need an origin story. The most popular hypothesis is that a small icy moon, perhaps 100 kilometers across, wandered too close to Saturn and was torn apart by tidal forces, producing the debris that now forms the rings. An alternative is that a passing comet was disrupted and captured by Saturn's gravity. Neither scenario has been conclusively established.

The rings are also disappearing. The ring material is slowly being pulled into Saturn as "ring rain," a fine mist of icy particles falling onto the planet's upper atmosphere. At current rates, the rings could be gone in a few hundred million years. We may be looking at a temporary, recent feature of the solar system — which means the question "why now?" becomes unavoidable. Why do we happen to be observing Saturn during the brief interval in its long life when it has rings? Is this coincidence, or does it indicate that ring systems are common on giant planets but short-lived?

## 14. Fast-Spinning Supermassive Black Holes — galactic cores spinning faster than physics expected

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Black_hole_-_Messier_87_crop_max_res.jpg/320px-Black_hole_-_Messier_87_crop_max_res.jpg){alt=""}
*The supermassive black hole at the heart of galaxy M87, imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019. It weighs about 6.5 billion times the mass of the Sun and spins at a large fraction of the theoretical maximum.*


*Black hole astrophysics, 2000s onward*

At the center of most large galaxies sits a supermassive black hole — typically millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun. The Milky Way's central black hole, Sagittarius A*, is about 4 million solar masses. The one at the center of the galaxy M87, photographed by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019, is about 6.5 billion solar masses.

Black holes have two main properties: mass and spin. Spin is measured on a scale from zero (not spinning) to one (spinning at the maximum rate allowed by physics). By analyzing the X-ray emissions from hot gas falling into them, astronomers can estimate spin. The results have been surprising.

Many supermassive black holes are spinning remarkably fast — close to the theoretical maximum. This is puzzling because the process of feeding a black hole through a disk of gas tends to moderate its spin over time. Material spiraling in can add or subtract angular momentum, depending on how it arrives, and statistical arguments suggest that most supermassive black holes should have intermediate spins, not extreme ones.

The high observed spin rates suggest that supermassive black holes grow primarily through a small number of coherent, large accretion events rather than through many random smaller ones. This affects models of how galaxies and their central black holes co-evolve over cosmic time. The picture of supermassive black hole growth is being actively revised in light of these observations.

## 15. The Missing Baryons — where half the universe's ordinary matter was hiding

*Cosmology, 1990s; resolved 2020*

For decades, cosmologists had a problem with ordinary matter — the normal protons and neutrons that make up stars, planets, and people. The total amount of ordinary matter predicted by Big Bang theory, and confirmed by measurements of the cosmic microwave background, was well known. But when astronomers added up the visible matter in stars, gas clouds, and galaxy clusters, they could only account for about half of it. The rest — the missing baryons — seemed to be somewhere nobody could find.

The suspected hiding place was the warm–hot intergalactic medium, or WHIM: a vast, thin plasma of gas spread through the filaments of the cosmic web, connecting galaxy clusters. But the WHIM is so tenuous and so faint that direct detection was extremely difficult.

Fast Radio Bursts — the same findings described in an earlier entry — turned out to be the solution. As a radio burst travels across the universe, its different wavelengths slow down by different amounts depending on how many free electrons they pass through. By measuring this dispersion for FRBs at known distances, astronomers could weigh the gas along each burst's path. In 2020, a team led by Jean-Pierre Macquart published the results of this technique applied to multiple well-localized FRBs. The total amount of gas measured matched the missing baryon prediction almost exactly.

This is one of the cleaner resolutions in modern astronomy: an anomaly solved by using another anomaly as a tool. The missing baryons were hiding in plain sight, spread through the vast, dim, hot plasma between galaxies. FRBs gave us the flashlight we needed to see them.

## 16. The Hubble Tension — two methods, two answers, one universe

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/Universe_expansion.png/320px-Universe_expansion.png){alt=""}
*The expansion of the universe over time. Two independent ways of measuring the current expansion rate — the Hubble constant — give answers that disagree at the 5-sigma level.*


*Cosmology, 2010s onward*

The Hubble constant measures how fast the universe is expanding today. It is one of the most fundamental numbers in cosmology. There are two main ways to measure it.

The first uses the cosmic microwave background — the light from the young universe — to measure the expansion rate as it was 380,000 years after the Big Bang, then uses the standard cosmological model to calculate what the current rate should be. This method, anchored by data from the Planck satellite, gives a value of about 67.4 kilometers per second per megaparsec.

The second uses "standard candles" in the nearby universe — stars with known brightness, like Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae — to directly measure the expansion rate today. This method, anchored by decades of careful work led by astronomer Adam Riess, gives a value of about 73.0.

The two numbers disagree by about 8 percent. That might not sound like much, but the error bars on each measurement have shrunk to the point where the disagreement is now a 5-sigma detection — statistically equivalent to flipping a coin 25 times and getting the same face every time. Either one of the measurements is wrong in a way that has resisted decades of attempts to find it, or the standard cosmological model is missing something fundamental.

Many solutions have been proposed: early dark energy that briefly dominated the universe before becoming negligible, modified gravity, new particle species, and changes to the physics of the early universe. None has been universally accepted. The James Webb Space Telescope has provided its own independent measurements of the local expansion rate using several different standard-candle techniques. The results have largely confirmed the disagreement rather than resolving it.

The Hubble tension is currently one of the most actively debated problems in cosmology. Its resolution may require a genuine revision of the standard model of the universe — or it may come down to a subtle systematic error nobody has yet found. As of 2026, nobody knows which.

## 17. 'Oumuamua — the cigar-shaped interstellar visitor that accelerated on its way out

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Oumuamua-ESO.jpg/320px-Oumuamua-ESO.jpg){alt=""}
*An artist's rendering of 'Oumuamua, the first interstellar object ever detected passing through our solar system, based on its measured tumbling light curve.*


*Solar system astronomy, 2017*

In October 2017, the Pan-STARRS telescope in Hawaii spotted something moving faster than should be possible for any object bound to the Sun. Within days, astronomers confirmed that its trajectory was hyperbolic — meaning the object was not on a closed orbit around the Sun but was passing through the solar system from interstellar space. It was the first interstellar object ever detected.

They named it 'Oumuamua, a Hawaiian word meaning roughly "scout" or "messenger from afar." It was already on its way out when it was found, and telescopes had only weeks to observe it before it became too faint to study.

What they saw was strange. 'Oumuamua was extremely elongated — possibly ten times longer than it was wide, more like a cigar or a pancake than a typical asteroid or comet. Its brightness varied dramatically as it tumbled. It showed no visible tail or coma, so it did not appear to be a comet. But as it moved, it accelerated — gaining speed in a way that gravity alone could not explain. A small but real non-gravitational push was acting on it.

Comets routinely experience this kind of push from gases evaporating off their surfaces as they warm near the Sun. But 'Oumuamua showed no detectable gas or dust. What was pushing it?

Several explanations have been proposed. Hydrogen or nitrogen ice, invisible to telescopes, might have been outgassing from the object. It might be a fragment of a nitrogen-rich exoplanet shattered in some ancient impact. Astrophysicist Avi Loeb famously proposed that it might be an artifact of an extraterrestrial civilization — a light sail, perhaps. The mainstream view is that some natural explanation will eventually prove correct, but no single proposal has been fully convincing. 'Oumuamua remains the first known interstellar visitor, and also one of the strangest.

## 18. The KBC Void — the billion-light-year empty region that might contain our galaxy

*Cosmology, 2013*

In 2013, astronomers Keenan, Barger, and Cowie — from whose initials the void takes its name — published evidence that our galaxy sits inside an enormous, relatively empty region of space. The KBC Void is estimated to be about two billion light-years across, making it one of the largest voids known.

This is unusual. The standard cosmological model predicts that the universe is roughly homogeneous — that matter is distributed evenly on very large scales. Small voids and clumps are expected, but a void as large as the KBC Void is at the edge of what the model comfortably allows.

If the KBC Void is real, it may have practical consequences. Recent analyses have suggested that sitting inside such a large underdensity would make local measurements of the cosmic expansion rate slightly higher than the true average — which could partially contribute to the Hubble tension described in an earlier entry.

The evidence for the void's existence has strengthened over time. Infrared galaxy surveys, counts of visible galaxies, and analyses of the cosmic microwave background all show consistent patterns. But whether the void is deep enough and large enough to produce a meaningful effect on cosmological measurements is still debated. This is a case where astronomical observations about our local environment connect directly to the largest-scale questions in cosmology.

## 19. JWST's Impossibly Early Galaxies — galaxies that grew up too fast for the standard model

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/James_Webb_Space_Telescope_Illustration_%28cropped%29.jpg/320px-James_Webb_Space_Telescope_Illustration_%28cropped%29.jpg){alt=""}
*The James Webb Space Telescope, whose observations of the early universe have shown galaxies more mature and massive than the standard cosmological model predicts.*


*Cosmology, 2022 onward*

When the James Webb Space Telescope began returning science data in mid-2022, one of its main goals was to find the earliest galaxies in the universe. It quickly found them — and they were much bigger and more mature than anyone expected.

Galaxies observed from within the first 500 million years after the Big Bang have turned out to be unexpectedly massive, unexpectedly bright, and unexpectedly structured. Some have well-formed disks. Some have already built up large amounts of stars. This is difficult to square with how galaxies are supposed to form.

The standard model of cosmology — called Lambda-CDM — makes specific predictions about how fast galaxies can grow. Gravity can only pull matter together so quickly. Dark matter halos take time to build up. The early universe should not yet have the infrastructure to support galaxies of this size and maturity.

Two kinds of solutions are being explored. The first says that galaxy formation physics is more efficient in the early universe than models assumed — perhaps supernovae were less disruptive to star formation then, allowing more gas to be converted into stars faster. The second says the problem runs deeper and points toward a flaw in the cosmological framework itself. Some researchers have suggested that the same modification to the early universe that could resolve the Hubble tension might also explain why galaxies formed faster than expected.

As of 2026, the debate is unresolved and intensifying as more data arrives.

## 20. The Ho'oleilana Structure — a single pressure wave from the Big Bang, frozen for 13 billion years

*Cosmology, 2023*

Sound travels through the universe. Not sound as we experience it — you cannot hear anything in space — but pressure waves moving through the hot plasma of the early universe, before atoms had even formed. These waves moved at more than half the speed of light and left a frozen imprint in the distribution of matter when the universe cooled enough for atoms to form, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang.

That imprint shows up as a slight tendency for galaxies to be separated by a characteristic distance — about 490 million light-years. It is called the Baryon Acoustic Oscillation scale, and it has been measured in galaxy surveys as a "standard ruler" for mapping the universe. The pattern is consistent, reliable, and well understood.

In September 2023, astronomer Brent Tully and his collaborators announced they had found what appeared to be a single, spherical shell of galaxies roughly one billion light-years across — a single fossilized baryon acoustic oscillation, visible as a coherent structure in the galaxy distribution around our cosmic neighborhood. They named it Ho'oleilana, a Hawaiian phrase from a chant about the dawning of the universe.

Here is the problem: the standard model predicts that the universe becomes smooth and featureless — homogeneous — at scales larger than about 1.2 billion light-years. Ho'oleilana, at one billion light-years across, is pushing right up against that limit. If it is a coherent structure, it challenges the assumption that the universe averages out to uniformity at large scales.

The scientific community is divided on how seriously to take it. Some argue that selecting a particular sphere centered on our neighborhood introduces a bias. Others believe Ho'oleilana is real and joins a growing list of structures that collectively suggest the universe is less uniform at large scales than the standard model predicts.

## 21. Comet 2I/Borisov — chemistry from a foreign solar system, familiar as our own

*Solar system astronomy, 2019*

When 'Oumuamua passed through in 2017, astronomers wanted more. They wanted to catch the next interstellar visitor earlier, study it longer, and understand what it was made of. They got their chance in 2019.

Amateur astronomer Gennady Borisov discovered a moving object on August 30, 2019. Within days, orbital calculations confirmed it was on a hyperbolic path — coming from outside the solar system. Unlike 'Oumuamua, this one was unambiguously a comet: it had a coma (a fuzzy cloud of gas and dust surrounding a solid nucleus) and a developing tail.

Telescopes around the world trained on it. What they found was both reassuring and thought-provoking. Borisov's chemistry was remarkably similar to comets from our own solar system. It contained carbon monoxide, water ice, and silicate dust in proportions that any solar system comet might have. This suggested that the raw materials of planetary systems — and potentially the building blocks of life — are not unique to our corner of the galaxy. Basic planetary chemistry may be widespread.

Borisov provided a useful contrast to 'Oumuamua's strangeness and set a baseline expectation for interstellar visitors. Then along came 3I/ATLAS and shattered the baseline.

## 22. 3I/ATLAS — the third interstellar visitor, chemically unlike anything in our solar system

*Solar system astronomy, 2025*

On July 1, 2025, a telescope in Chile belonging to the ATLAS survey program spotted an object moving fast across the sky. Very fast. Orbital calculations confirmed within hours: this was only the third interstellar object ever detected, and it was approaching the inner solar system. Astronomers around the world immediately turned their telescopes toward it.

3I/ATLAS — the "3I" standing for "third interstellar" — was clearly a comet. But its chemistry was unlike anything expected. It was rich in carbon dioxide ice, produced nickel vapor in quantities not seen from solar system comets, and showed unusual water activity. Compared to Borisov, it was chemically alien.

The differences tell a story. 3I/ATLAS likely formed farther from its parent star than Borisov did, in colder conditions where carbon dioxide and other volatile ices could accumulate. The nickel vapor pointed to unusual chemical processing or a different dust composition than our solar system's comets carry. Each interstellar visitor is essentially a sample returned from another planetary system — one that we can never visit, but whose chemistry travels to us.

With three interstellar objects now detected — one deeply anomalous, one familiar, one alien — astronomers are beginning to sketch the outlines of a larger picture: planetary chemistry varies significantly from system to system. The Vera Rubin Observatory, in its ongoing all-sky survey, is expected to detect many more interstellar visitors in the coming years, building a statistical picture of what the galaxy's planetary systems are made of.

## 23. Rogue Planets — billions of worlds drifting through the galaxy without a sun

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/Free_floating_planet_%28artist_impression%29.jpg/320px-Free_floating_planet_%28artist_impression%29.jpg){alt=""}
*An artist's rendering of a rogue planet — a world drifting through the galaxy without a star. Rogue planets may outnumber stars in the Milky Way.*


*Astrobiology, 2011*

Not every planet orbits a star. Some worlds drift through the galaxy alone, in permanent darkness, flung out of their home solar systems during the violent gravitational chaos of planetary formation. These are called rogue planets, and they are far more common than anyone expected.

The evidence comes from a phenomenon called gravitational microlensing. When a massive object — including a small, dark planet — passes in front of a distant background star, its gravity acts as a lens, briefly brightening the star's light in a characteristic pattern. By monitoring millions of stars and counting these lensing events, astronomers can estimate how many rogue planets are out there.

A major survey published in 2011 suggested there might be about two Jupiter-mass rogue planets for every star in the galaxy — roughly 200 billion of them in the Milky Way alone. More recent surveys have pushed the estimate even higher, particularly for smaller, Earth-mass rogues that are harder to detect. Some analyses suggest rogue planets could outnumber stars.

Most of these worlds are frozen, dark, and lifeless. But there is a minority possibility worth taking seriously: some rogue planets might retain heat from radioactive decay in their rocky interiors. On some worlds, this could be enough to keep subsurface oceans of liquid water from freezing, even in the deep cold of interstellar space. This is not a comfortable environment for life as we know it. But it is not physically impossible. The universe may contain billions of permanently wandering worlds that we have barely started to characterize, some of which may have liquid water. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, expected to launch by 2027, will conduct a comprehensive microlensing survey that will substantially sharpen the population estimate.

## 24. Is Dark Energy Weakening? — the cosmological constant may not be constant after all

*Cosmology, 2024 and 2025*

We have known since 1998 that the universe's expansion is accelerating. We do not know why. The simplest explanation — that empty space has a fixed, built-in energy density called the cosmological constant — is consistent with all the data collected over the following 25 years. Dark energy has appeared constant. In 2024, the first hints arrived that it might not be.

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, or DESI, is a remarkable piece of technology. Mounted on a telescope in Arizona, it can measure the spectra of 5,000 galaxies simultaneously, using 5,000 small robots that reposition fiber optic cables to capture light from different targets in under two minutes. Over its planned ten-year survey, it will map the three-dimensional positions of tens of millions of galaxies across a large fraction of the observable universe.

In 2024, DESI released its first-year results. The data suggested that dark energy may have been stronger in the past and may be weakening now. In March 2025, the three-year results confirmed the trend using a dataset of 13.1 million galaxies — the most detailed 3D map of the universe ever constructed.

The signal is real, but it is not yet strong enough to be certain. The DESI team described it as suggestive, not conclusive. The full ten-year dataset, expected by the early 2030s, will determine whether dark energy is truly changing or whether the current signal is a statistical fluctuation. ESA's Euclid satellite and the Rubin Observatory are also gathering complementary data that will sharpen the picture.

If dark energy is changing, the cosmological constant is wrong, and the standard model's most fundamental assumption about the fate of the universe needs to be revised.

## 25. The S8 Tension — matter clustered more slowly than the standard model predicts

*Cosmology, 2010s onward*

The cosmic microwave background is a snapshot of the universe 380,000 years after the Big Bang. The tiny temperature variations in it represent density variations — regions slightly denser or less dense than average. Gravity amplified those differences over billions of years into the galaxies, clusters, and cosmic web we see today.

Using the CMB as a starting point, cosmologists can predict exactly how clumpy — how much matter is clustered together — the universe should be today. They express this prediction as a number called S8.

The problem is that when astronomers directly measure how clumpy the universe actually is today, using a technique called weak gravitational lensing — how light from distant galaxies is subtly distorted by the matter between them and us — the universe comes out smoother than predicted. Not dramatically smoother. About 2 to 3 percent smoother. But this discrepancy has now been confirmed by multiple independent surveys using different telescopes, different parts of the sky, and different analysis techniques.

Like the Hubble tension, the S8 tension is relatively modest in isolation but deeply uncomfortable in context. It suggests that matter may have been somewhat slower to cluster than the standard model predicts — perhaps because dark matter is not quite as cold and sluggish as assumed, or because dark matter particles interact with each other in ways not included in current models.

The two tensions — Hubble and S8 — are linked. Proposed solutions to one often make the other worse. Finding a model that resolves both simultaneously has become one of the central challenges in theoretical cosmology.

## 26. The Quipu Superstructure — a cosmic structure too large for the universe to contain

*Cosmology, 2025*

In early 2025, a team of astronomers using the eROSITA X-ray telescope announced the discovery of the largest cosmic structure ever identified. They named it Quipu, after the knotted cords that the Inca used to record information — because it resembled a long, branching string of knots spread across space.

Quipu is a chain of 68 galaxy clusters — each cluster itself containing hundreds of galaxies — linked together in three-dimensional space. Its minimum length is 1.4 billion light-years. Its mass is approximately 240 million billion times the mass of the Sun.

Here is why this matters. Cosmological theory predicts that the universe becomes homogeneous — smooth and featureless, with no coherent structures — above a scale of about 1.2 billion light-years. This is called the homogeneity scale, and it is a fundamental prediction of the standard model. Quipu stretches 1.4 billion light-years — past that limit.

Quipu is not alone in this. The Giant Arc, discovered in 2021, stretches about 3.3 billion light-years. The Hercules-Corona Borealis Great Wall may span up to 10 billion light-years, though its coherence as a single structure is disputed. A growing number of large-scale structures seem to push past the point where the universe is supposed to be smooth.

Astronomers are not yet certain how seriously to take these findings. Some structures that appear coherent in 2D projections on the sky turn out to be less impressive in 3D. Statistical effects can make random distributions appear patterned to the human eye. These concerns are being investigated.

But if the trend holds — if the universe truly has coherent structures larger than the homogeneity scale — the cosmological principle, which is not an observation but an assumption baked into every cosmological model, may need to be reconsidered. And reconsidering it would require rebuilding much of modern cosmology from the foundation up.

## Conclusion

Twenty-six findings. Nearly a century of observations. Several themes run through all of them.

**The standard model of cosmology is under pressure from multiple directions at once.** The Hubble tension, the S8 tension, the DESI dark energy signal, JWST's early galaxies, the growing catalogue of too-large structures: any one of these might turn out to have a boring explanation. All of them persisting together is much harder to dismiss. This is how paradigm shifts begin — not with a single dramatic discovery, but with a quiet accumulation of inconsistencies that the prevailing theory cannot quite absorb.

**Most of the universe is made of things we do not understand.** Dark matter and dark energy together account for roughly 95 percent of the universe's total energy content. We infer them from their effects, but we do not know what they are. Fifty years of searching for dark matter particles have produced nothing. Twenty-five years of cosmological observations have not told us whether dark energy is a fixed property of space or something that changes. We live inside a universe we mostly cannot see.

**Some anomalies turn out to be new physics, and some turn out to be waste heat.** The Pioneer anomaly looked for three decades like a window onto new forces of nature. It turned out to be thermal radiation from the spacecraft itself. The missing baryons looked like a deep problem with cosmological accounting. They turned out to be hiding in plain sight, measurable once fast radio bursts became available as a probe. Every anomaly in this book could go either way. The work is in finding out which.

**The question of life in the universe has moved from philosophy to science.** The Fermi Paradox is no longer just an interesting puzzle for late-night conversations. It is being tested by instruments capable of detecting molecules like oxygen or methane in the atmospheres of planets dozens of light-years away. The Wow! Signal remains unexplained. Interstellar comets are bringing us chemical samples from other solar systems. Rogue planets may host subsurface oceans in the darkness between stars. The discovery of even a single microbe anywhere other than Earth would change everything.

**The universe is stranger at the largest and smallest scales than anyone expected.** Structures bigger than the homogeneity scale should not exist. Supermassive black holes should not spin as fast as they do. Galaxies 500 million years after the Big Bang should not be as mature as they appear. An interstellar object should not accelerate without outgassing. The anomalies cluster at the scales where the standard picture makes its strongest predictions — which is exactly where new physics, if it is hiding anywhere, would be hardest to conceal.

The universe has never been more comprehensively observed. The fact that it is also more puzzling than ever is not a failure. It is an invitation.

## Glossary

**Antimatter.** The mirror counterpart of ordinary matter. When matter and antimatter meet, they annihilate, releasing energy. The universe contains almost no antimatter today, though equal amounts should have been produced at the Big Bang.

**Baryogenesis.** The unknown process that tipped the balance between matter and antimatter in the early universe, leaving the slight excess of matter from which everything is made.

**Baryon.** A particle made of three quarks, such as a proton or neutron. In cosmology, "baryonic matter" is used loosely to mean ordinary atomic matter, as distinguished from dark matter.

**Baryon acoustic oscillation.** A characteristic separation distance of about 490 million light-years between galaxies, left over as a frozen pressure wave from the early universe. Used by cosmologists as a standard ruler for measuring cosmic distances.

**Big Bang nucleosynthesis.** The production of the lightest atomic nuclei — hydrogen, helium, and small amounts of lithium — in the first minutes after the Big Bang.

**Biosignature.** A chemical indicator in a planet's atmosphere — such as oxygen or methane — that could indicate the presence of life.

**Black hole.** A region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. Supermassive black holes, with millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun, sit at the centers of most large galaxies.

**Cosmic microwave background (CMB).** The faint glow of light left over from the Big Bang, emitted when the universe was about 380,000 years old and first cooled enough to become transparent.

**Cosmic web.** The pattern by which matter is organized at the largest scales: galaxy clusters strung along filaments and sheets, with large empty voids between them.

**Cosmological constant.** A fixed amount of energy in every cubic centimeter of empty space. The simplest candidate for dark energy, and the subject of a severe theoretical discrepancy between prediction and observation.

**Cosmological principle.** The assumption, baked into every standard cosmological model, that the universe is approximately uniform on the largest scales — the same in every direction and at every location, averaged over enough space.

**Dark energy.** A mysterious property of space itself that is pushing the universe apart faster and faster. Accounts for about 68 percent of the universe's total energy content.

**Dark matter.** Invisible matter that outweighs ordinary matter by about five to one. Known only through its gravitational effects. Its physical identity remains unknown.

**Fast radio burst (FRB).** A brief, extremely bright flash of radio waves from deep space. Some repeat, some do not. At least some are produced by magnetars.

**Fermi Paradox.** The apparent contradiction between the high likelihood of extraterrestrial civilizations in a galaxy as large and old as ours, and the total absence of evidence for any of them.

**Great Filter.** The hypothetical step in the development of intelligent life that almost nothing gets past. Whether this filter lies in our past or our future is a matter of speculation.

**Gravitational lensing.** The bending of light from distant objects by the gravity of massive objects in the foreground. "Weak lensing" refers to subtle distortions of background galaxies used to map the distribution of matter. "Microlensing" refers to brief brightenings caused by small foreground objects, including planets.

**Homogeneity scale.** The size above which the universe is predicted to be uniform and featureless — roughly 1.2 billion light-years. Structures larger than this challenge the cosmological principle.

**Hubble constant.** The current rate of expansion of the universe. Two different methods of measuring it currently give conflicting values.

**Hubble tension.** The statistically significant disagreement between measurements of the Hubble constant made from the cosmic microwave background and measurements made from the nearby universe.

**Inflation (cosmic).** The proposed brief period of exponential expansion in the first tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang, believed to explain why the universe is so uniform and so flat.

**Interstellar object.** An object passing through our solar system on a trajectory indicating it originated around another star. Three are known as of 2026: 'Oumuamua, 2I/Borisov, and 3I/ATLAS.

**Lambda-CDM.** The standard model of cosmology. "Lambda" refers to the cosmological constant; "CDM" stands for cold dark matter.

**Magnetar.** A neutron star with an extraordinarily powerful magnetic field. Identified as a source of at least some fast radio bursts.

**MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics).** A hypothesis that seeks to explain galactic rotation curves by modifying gravity at large scales rather than introducing dark matter.

**Neutron star.** The extremely dense remnant of a massive star after a supernova. Typically 20 kilometers across but with more mass than the Sun.

**Redshift.** The stretching of light wavelengths by the expansion of the universe. Used to measure the distance of objects.

**S8.** A cosmological parameter describing how clumpy the universe's matter is today. Predicted from the CMB and measured directly via weak lensing; the two values currently disagree modestly.

**SETI.** The Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence. An umbrella term for scientific efforts to detect signals from technological civilizations beyond Earth.

**Standard candle.** An astronomical object whose intrinsic brightness is known, allowing its distance to be inferred from its observed brightness. Type Ia supernovae are the most important standard candles for cosmology.

**Supermassive black hole.** A black hole with a mass of millions to billions of times the mass of the Sun. Sits at the center of most large galaxies.

**Supernova.** The explosive death of a massive star. Type Ia supernovae, a specific subtype, are used as standard candles for measuring cosmic distances.

**Type Ia supernova.** A supernova with a predictable peak brightness, used as a distance marker in cosmology.

**WHIM (warm–hot intergalactic medium).** A tenuous plasma of hot gas spread through the filaments of the cosmic web, connecting galaxy clusters. Long believed to contain the universe's "missing" baryons — a belief confirmed by FRB measurements in 2020.

**WIMP (weakly interacting massive particle).** A hypothetical particle long considered the leading candidate for dark matter. Despite decades of searching, no WIMP has been detected.

**Zone of Avoidance.** The region of the sky obscured by the dust and gas of our own galaxy's disk, through which optical telescopes cannot see. Requires infrared, radio, or X-ray observations.
